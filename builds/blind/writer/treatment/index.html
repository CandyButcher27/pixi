01 — How it reads

Seven passes, in the order an editor makes them.

Structure first, commas last. Each pass runs on its own and only surfaces notes above your confidence threshold, so a 4,000-word essay averages 23 notes, not 230.

  1. Argument~1.2 s

    Does each section earn its place? Flags claims made twice and claims never supported.

    As noted above, remote work raises output. Claim repeated from ¶2 — cut or add evidence

  2. Order~0.9 s

    Suggests moving paragraphs, with the reason: "this answers a question you have not asked yet".

    Move ¶6 before ¶4 — it defines "slack" first

  3. Evidence~2.4 s

    Marks numbers, dates and quotes that need a source, and links the ones it can verify.

    "up 47% since 2019" source? BLS table 3 matches 41%

  4. Rhythm~0.6 s

    Spots runs of same-length sentences and paragraphs that all open the same way.

    5 sentences of 11–13 words in a row

  5. Diction~0.7 s

    Hedges, filler and words your house style bans. Shows the count, not just the instance.

    very unique unique · "basically" ×6

  6. Clarity~0.8 s

    Sentences over 38 words, buried verbs, pronouns with two possible owners.

    it the council — two possible owners

  7. Mechanics~0.3 s

    Spelling, punctuation, agreement. Last, because fixing commas in a paragraph you will cut is wasted work.

    its it's · alot a lot

03 — House style

It learns your voice, not a template's.

Feed it three pieces you are proud of. It writes down the rules it found — as a style sheet you can read and edit — and checks new drafts against those, not against an average of the internet.
